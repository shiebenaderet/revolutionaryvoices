Abigail Adams

A Letter from Braintree

Born: November 22, 1744, Weymouth, Massachusetts Died: October 28, 1818 (age 73) Role: Letter-writer who ran the farm; later the president's wife Famous for: "Remember the Ladies" letter (1776); more than 1,100 letters; opposing slavery

On March 31, 1776, Abigail Adams sat down to write a letter. She was at her farm in Braintree, Massachusetts. Her husband, John Adams, was far away in Philadelphia. He was at the Continental Congress. That was the meeting of leaders from all the colonies. She asked him if Congress had voted for independence yet. Independence means America would be its own country. Then she told him what she wanted in the new laws.

A Minister's Daughter

Abigail Smith was born on November 22, 1744. She grew up in Weymouth, Massachusetts. Her father was the town minister. Schools then were for boys. So she never went to school. She read the books in her father's library. In 1764 she married John Adams. He was a young lawyer.

Running the Farm

In 1774 John Adams went to Congress. After that he was away for years at a time. Abigail Adams stayed home. She raised the children. She ran the farm and the family's money.

The war came close to her home. On June 17, 1775, she heard cannons from Braintree. It was the Battle of Bunker Hill. The next day she wrote to John. She told him their friend Dr. Joseph Warren had been killed. She wrote that Charlestown "is laid in ashes."

The two wrote to each other all the time. More than 1,100 of their letters still exist.

Remember the Ladies

In her letter of March 31, 1776, she asked John to think of women. "Remember the Ladies," she wrote. She said husbands should not have all the power. She said women would not obey laws they had no voice in. The Patriots said the same thing about Britain.

John wrote back on April 14. He said he could only laugh. He called her "saucy." That summer the Declaration of Independence was signed. It said nothing about women's rights.

Against Slavery

Adams also wrote against slavery. In slavery, a person is owned by someone else. In 1774 she wrote that it was wrong to fight for freedom and take it from others. In 1797 a free Black boy named James went to a night school. A neighbor did not want him there. Adams stood up for the boy. She said he was as free as any of the young men.

First Lady

In 1797 John Adams became the second president. Abigail Adams was the First Lady. The First Lady is the wife of the president. She held that job until 1801. Later, women who wanted the right to vote used her words. Abigail Adams died at home in Quincy on October 28, 1818. She was 73.

"Remember the Ladies" — the letter itself Braintree, March 31, 1776

Abigail Adams wrote this letter on March 31, 1776. She wrote it at home in Braintree, Massachusetts. She sent it to her husband, John Adams. He was at the Continental Congress in Philadelphia. She wanted the new laws to be fairer to women. A tyrant is a ruler with no limits. John wrote back that he had to laugh. He called her "saucy."

We will not obey any laws that we had no voice in making.

New England schools of that time were for boys, so she never went to school. She learned at home, reading the books in her father's library, and her spelling stayed her own for the rest of her life, because English spelling was not yet standard and she had taught herself. On October 25, 1764, a month before her twentieth birthday, she married John Adams, a young lawyer from Braintree.

Running the Farm

In 1774 John Adams went to Philadelphia as a delegate to the First Continental Congress. For most of the next ten years he was away, first in Congress and later in Europe as a diplomat, an official who represents his country abroad. Abigail Adams stayed in Braintree, raised their four children, and ran the farm and the family's money. She kept the family's accounts and reported to John on prices, taxes and the harvest.

The war came close to her home. On June 17, 1775, she heard the cannon of the Battle of Bunker Hill from Braintree. The next day she wrote to John: "The Day; perhaps the decisive Day is come on which the fate of America depends." She told him that their friend Dr. Joseph Warren had been killed, that "Charlstown is laid in ashes," and that "the constant roar of the cannon is so distressing that we can not Eat, Drink or Sleep." Their son John Quincy Adams, then seven years old, later recalled that he saw the fires with his own eyes.

The two wrote to each other constantly. The Massachusetts Historical Society holds 1,160 letters between them, written from 1762 to 1801, and the family saved them. They are one of the best records historians have of family life during the Revolution.

Remember the Ladies

In her letter of March 31, 1776, she asked John to keep women in mind when Congress wrote the laws of the new country. "I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors," she wrote. "Do not put such unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands. Remember all Men would be tyrants if they could." She warned that if the laws ignored women, "we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation." The Patriots had made the same argument against Britain, that Parliament could not tax people who had no representation, no one to speak for them in the government. Under the laws of the time, a married woman's property and earnings were controlled by her husband, and no woman in Massachusetts could vote.

John Adams answered on April 14. "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh," he wrote. He said he had heard that the war had loosened every kind of authority, that "Indians slighted their Guardians and Negroes grew insolent to their Masters," but that her letter was the first hint "that another Tribe more numerous and powerfull than all the rest were grown discontented." He joked that giving up the men's authority would subject them "to the Despotism of the Peticoat," and he added, "you are so saucy, I wont blot it out." That summer the Declaration of Independence said nothing about women's rights.

Against Slavery

Adams also wrote against slavery. In September 1774 she told John that it had "allways appeard a most iniquitious Scheme to me" to fight for liberty while "daily robbing and plundering" it from people who had as good a right to it. In February 1797 a free Black boy named James began attending Master Heath's evening school in Quincy. A neighbor named Faxon objected to his being there. Adams went to Faxon and defended the boy. "The Boy is a Freeman as much as any of the young Men," she wrote on February 13, "and merely because his Face is Black, is he to be denied instruction."

John Adams became the second president of the United States in 1797, and Abigail Adams was the First Lady, the wife of the president, from 1797 to 1801. In later years, women who campaigned for the right to vote quoted her 1776 letter in their own speeches and books. Abigail Adams died of typhoid fever at home in Quincy on October 28, 1818, at the age of 73.
