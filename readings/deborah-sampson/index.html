Deborah Sampson

Disguised herself as a man to serve in the American army

Born: December 17, 1760, Plympton, Massachusetts Died: April 29, 1827 (age 66) Role: American soldier under the name "Robert Shurtliff" Famous for: Serving about 17 months disguised as a man; among the first women to get army pay

Deborah Sampson was born on December 17, 1760, in Plympton, Massachusetts. Her family was poor, and as a child she became an indentured servant, working for years without pay. The hard farm labor made her tall and strong. She also taught herself to read.

Deborah wanted to serve in the Revolutionary War, but women were not allowed to be soldiers. So in 1782 she cut her hair, made men's clothes, and enlisted in the Continental Army under the name "Robert Shurtliff." She joined the 4th Massachusetts Regiment and served for about a year and a half. She marched, scouted, and fought in (small, quick battles), and she was wounded in the leg.

In 1783 Deborah became very ill with a fever in Philadelphia. A doctor discovered she was a woman. Instead of punishing her, the army gave her an (an official, respectful release from the army). After the war she married, raised a family, and gave public talks about her service — unusual for a woman then. She became one of the first women to receive a military pension. She died in 1827.

Deborah Sampson was born on December 17, 1760, in Plympton, Massachusetts, into a poor family. After her father abandoned them, she was bound out as an (a worker bound by contract for a set term). Years of hard farm labor left her unusually tall and strong. She taught herself to read and later worked briefly as a teacher.

Women were barred from the army, but Sampson was determined to join the Revolutionary cause. In 1782 she enlisted in the Continental Army under the name "Robert Shurtliff." She served in the 4th Massachusetts Regiment, a light-infantry unit that did dangerous scouting and raiding. She served roughly seventeen months and was wounded in a (a small, quick fight) near Tarrytown, New York. By the traditional account, she even removed a musket ball from her own leg rather than let a doctor discover her secret.

Her identity finally came out in 1783, when she fell seriously ill in Philadelphia. A physician, Dr. Barnabas Binney, learned she was a woman. The army gave her an honorable discharge rather than a punishment. After the war she married Benjamin Gannett and raised a family. In 1802 she became one of the first American women to tour as a public lecturer, sometimes performing military drills in uniform. She was among the first women to receive a military pension. After her death in 1827, her husband became the first man to receive a pension based on a wife's service.

Deborah Sampson — born December 17, 1760, in Plympton, Massachusetts — turned the rigid gender rules of her era into the cover for a remarkable enlistment. Bound out as an indentured servant after her father deserted the family, she grew tall and strong from farm labor, taught herself to read, and in 1782 enlisted in the Continental Army as "Robert Shurtliff," serving in the 4th Massachusetts Regiment for roughly seventeen months.

The most dramatic episodes of her story — digging a musket ball out of her own thigh near Tarrytown to avoid a doctor's examination, carrying secret messages — come largely from Herman Mann's 1797 biography "The Female Review" and from her own lecture tour, and historians caution that Mann (added invented details); surviving military records confirm her service and a wound but not every flourish. What is documented is striking enough: she lived and fought among men undetected until a fever in Philadelphia led Dr. Barnabas Binney to discover her sex, after which the army granted her an honorable discharge in 1783 rather than a punishment.

Sampson's afterlife was as pioneering as her service. In 1802 she became one of the first American women to lecture publicly for pay, appearing in uniform to perform the (the formal musket drill); with support from Paul Revere she petitioned for the back pay and pension the state and federal governments had withheld, and won a military pension in her own right. After her death in 1827, her husband Benjamin Gannett successfully petitioned Congress for a pension as the spouse of a soldier — the first man to do so. Her case forced a young republic to put on paper that a woman had, in fact, been a soldier.
