Haym Salomon

Mr. B's sample episode: a Jewish immigrant who helped prisoners escape and raised money for a broke government, and the legend that grew up around him

Born: 1740, Lissa, Poland, to a Jewish family Died: January 1785, Philadelphia (about age 44) Role: Merchant, prisoner of the British, then a money broker for Congress Famous for: A legend that he paid for the Revolution, which historians say is not true

Haym Salomon was born in Poland in 1740. His family was Jewish. He moved to New York and worked as a (a person who buys and sells goods).

In 1776, the British took over New York. They said Salomon was a spy. They put him in prison.

Salomon spoke many languages. So a German general working for the British put him to work. He got food and supplies for the officers. But in secret, he helped American and French prisoners escape.

In 1778, the British came after him again. He ran away to Philadelphia. He had to leave his wife and their baby behind. He lost almost everything he owned.

In Philadelphia, he started over. The new government was out of money. Salomon helped it turn paper promises into cash. He kept a small fee for his work. He also lent his own money to leaders who were broke.

Salomon died in 1785. Later, people said he paid for the whole war. They put it on a stamp. But historians checked the records. They say it is a (a story that grew bigger than the truth).

In September 1776 the British captured New York. They arrested Salomon as a spy and locked him in prison. Because he spoke German, a Hessian general put him to work buying supplies for the officers. That gave him some freedom. He used it to help American and French prisoners escape.

By August 1778 the British were after him again, and he escaped to Philadelphia. He left behind his wife, a month-old baby, and almost everything he owned. He asked Congress for a job, and it said no.

So he started over as a (someone paid a small fee to help others buy and sell). In 1781 Robert Morris took charge of the government's money and turned to Salomon. France and the Netherlands had loaned money as paper promises called bills of exchange. Salomon found buyers who paid cash for them. He also lent his own money to leaders like James Madison, sometimes without charging anything back.

Salomon died in January 1785. Years later his son told Congress that his father had lent the government huge sums. That claim became a legend, even on a 1975 stamp. Historians who checked the records say he never lent the government his own money.

Haym Salomon was born in 1740 in Lissa, Poland, to a Jewish family. He traveled across Europe, picked up several languages, and settled in New York in the early 1770s as a (someone who buys and sells goods for a living).

When the British captured New York in September 1776, they arrested Salomon as a suspected spy and held him in the city's military prison. His languages saved him: a Hessian general put him to work as a supplier for the officers. That job gave him enough freedom to work against the British from the inside, and he helped French and American prisoners escape. By August 1778 the British were after him again. He fled to Philadelphia, leaving his wife, a month-old child, and most of his property behind. In a request to Congress that month, he asked for any job in his line of business. Congress gave him none.

He rebuilt his life as a (someone who arranges sales for others and keeps a small fee). In 1781 Congress put Robert Morris in charge of its finances, and Morris used Salomon to sell bills of exchange, the paper promises that carried loans from France and the Netherlands. Salomon turned them into cash the government could spend. He also made personal loans, sometimes without interest, to members of Congress such as James Madison.

Salomon died in January 1785, about age 44. In the 1840s his son asked Congress to repay loans his father had supposedly made, first $353,000 and later $3 million. The story stuck. A 1975 stamp called him a "Financial Hero." But historians who studied Morris's records, in 1931 and again in 1977, found that Salomon never lent the government his own money and never claimed he had.

Haym Salomon's reputation has two lives: the documented career of a Polish-born Jewish broker who served the Revolution at real personal risk, and a nineteenth-century legend that credited him with financing it. Born in 1740 in Lissa, he arrived in New York in the early 1770s, and when the British occupied the city in September 1776 he was imprisoned as a suspected spy. His fluency in German led a Hessian commander to employ him as a (a supplier of provisions) to officers, a position he used to assist French and American prisoners in escaping.

Exposed again in August 1778, he fled to Philadelphia; his memorial to Congress that month describes the loss of his property and the wife and infant left behind, and asks only for employment. Refused, he became a bill broker, and after 1781 the Superintendent of Finance, Robert Morris, relied on him to convert foreign bills of exchange, the instruments carrying French and Dutch loans, into usable currency. He earned ordinary commissions, styled himself "Broker to the Office of Finance," and extended personal, sometimes interest-free, loans to impoverished delegates such as James Madison.

After his death in January 1785, his son petitioned Congress in the 1840s for repayment of fabulous sums, eventually $3 million, and the claim hardened into public memory: a proposed medal, a Chicago monument, a 1975 stamp. Max Kohler's 1931 study and Morris Schappes's work on the Morris diaries showed that Salomon lent the government nothing of his own. The more interesting question for a historian is why the legend proved so durable, and what it reveals about how communities claim a place in the national story.
