Haym Salomon

The Broker and the Legend

Born: 1740, Lissa, Poland, to a Jewish family Died: January 1785, Philadelphia (about age 44) Role: Merchant, prisoner of the British, then a money broker for Congress Famous for: A legend that he paid for the Revolution, which historians say is not true

In August 1778, a man named Haym Salomon wrote a letter to Congress. He had just escaped from New York. The British army held the city. He wrote that he had lost all he owned. He had left his wife and a tiny baby behind. He asked Congress for one thing, a job.

A Prisoner in New York

Salomon was born in Poland in 1740. His family was Jewish. He learned many languages. Then he moved to New York. He worked as a merchant. A merchant buys and sells goods.

In 1776 the British took New York. They said Salomon was a spy. They put him in prison. But a Hessian general needed a man who spoke many languages. Hessians were German soldiers paid to fight for Britain. The general let Salomon out to work for him. Salomon bought food and goods for the officers.

The job let him move around the city. So he helped American and French prisoners. He gave them money. He helped them escape. In 1778 the British came after him. He fled to Philadelphia.

Working for Robert Morris

Congress did not give him a job. So Salomon became a broker. A broker helps people buy and sell things. He keeps a small fee. In 1781 Robert Morris took charge of the country's money. The government had almost no cash. France had lent money to America. But the loan came as paper notes. Someone had to sell the notes for cash. Morris gave that job to Salomon. He sold about $200,000 worth of notes. He was paid a fee for each sale.

Salomon also lent his own money to some men in Congress. One was James Madison. Those were loans to the men. They were not loans to the government.

The Legend

Salomon died in January 1785. He was about 44. Years later, his son went to Congress. He said his father had lent the government a lot of money. He asked for $353,000. Later he asked for $3,000,000. Congress never paid. But the story spread. In 1975 a stamp called Salomon a "Financial Hero." It said he raised most of the money for the war.

Historians checked the records. They found no loans from Salomon to the government. He never said he made any. His own letter in 1778 asked only for a job.

On August 25, 1778, a merchant named Haym Salomon sent a memorial, a formal written request, to the Continental Congress in Philadelphia. He had escaped from British-held New York a few days before. The memorial, written in the third person as requests to Congress were, said that he had "most irrecoverably lost all his effects and credits to the amount of five or six thousand Pounds Sterling" and had left "his distressed wife and a child of a month old at New York." It asked Congress for one thing, "any Employ in the way of his business."

A Prisoner in New York

Salomon was born in 1740 in Lissa, Poland, to a Jewish family. He probably left Poland after 1772, when Russia, Prussia and Austria divided the country among themselves, and by 1776 he was living in New York as a merchant, a person who buys and sells goods for a living. In September 1776 the British army captured the city. According to his memorial, the British commandant, General James Robertson, soon had him taken up as a suspected spy and held in the Provost, the army's prison.

His languages got him out. The Hessian commander, Lieutenant General Leopold von Heister, wanted him, in the words of the memorial, "on account of his knowledge in the French, Polish, Russian, Italian etc. languages." Hessians were German soldiers whose rulers had hired them out to Britain. Heister had Salomon released into his service and made him a purveyor, a supplier of food and goods, "chiefly for the Officers." The job let Salomon move about the city, and he used that freedom against the army that employed him. His memorial says that he "assisted them with money and helped them off to make their escape," meaning the French and American prisoners held in New York.

By August 1778 the British were after him again. Salomon fled to Philadelphia, leaving behind his wife, Rachel Franks Salomon, their baby, and property he valued at five or six thousand pounds sterling. Congress took no action on his memorial.

Broker to the Office of Finance

Salomon started over as a broker, a person who arranges sales for others and keeps a small fee. Early in 1781 he advertised in Philadelphia as a dealer in bills of exchange on France and Amsterdam. Days later Congress named Robert Morris Superintendent of Finance, in charge of the nation's money. The government was nearly out of cash. France and the Netherlands had lent money to the United States, but the loans arrived as bills of exchange, paper orders for payment that had to be sold to merchants for cash before the money could be spent in America. Morris used Salomon to sell them. In 1781 and 1782 Salomon sold about $200,000 worth of bills and was paid a broker's fee. Morris's diary mentions him about seventy-five times between August 1781 and April 1784. On July 12, 1782, Salomon asked to advertise himself as "Broker to the Office of Finance," and Morris agreed, noting in the diary that "this broker has been useful to the public interests." Salomon also served as broker to the French consul and to the treasurer of the French army in America.

He lent his own money to members of Congress and other officials who were short of cash, among them James Madison, Edmund Randolph, Joseph Reed, Arthur St. Clair and Thomas Mifflin. Some of these loans charged no interest. They were private loans to the men, not loans to the government. Salomon was also a founding member of Philadelphia's synagogue, Mikveh Israel. In December 1783 he joined other members in a protest to the state's Council of Censors against the oath that barred Jews from holding office in Pennsylvania.

The Legend

Salomon died in Philadelphia on January 6, 1785, at about age 44, after a long illness. He left a widow and two small sons, Ezekiel and Haym M. Salomon. In 1846 the younger son began asking Congress to repay money he said his father had lent the government during the war. He first claimed $353,000, and over the years the figure grew to $3,000,000. Congressional committees reported in the family's favor more than once, but Congress never voted the money. In 1893 a House committee recommended a gold medal "in recognition of services rendered by Haym Salomon for advances of money," and that bill failed too. The story kept growing. In 1941 a bronze statue of Washington, Morris and Salomon was unveiled on Wacker Drive in Chicago. In 1975 the United States issued a ten-cent stamp captioned "Financial Hero," with a note on its back that Salomon "was responsible for raising most of the money needed to finance the American Revolution."

Checking the Records

Historians have tested the legend against the documents. In 1931 Max J. Kohler published a study of Salomon's career and found that he never lent the government money and never claimed to. Morris U. Schappes went through every reference to Salomon in Robert Morris's diaries. He presented his findings in 1950 and published them in 1977, and he described Salomon as a broker who sold government securities for a commission. Salomon, he wrote, "did not himself lend money to the government and never claimed he did." The evidence for the legend comes from the family's claims in the 1840s and after, not from any record of the 1780s. Salomon's own memorial of August 25, 1778, mentions no loans to the government. It describes a prisoner who helped other prisoners escape and a refugee who had lost everything, and it asks only for a job.
