Hessian Soldiers

German Soldiers in the British Army

Who: German soldiers, many from Hesse-Kassel, sent by their rulers How many: About 30,000 crossed the ocean. At times they were nearly 1 in 3 of Britain's soldiers. Why "rented": Britain paid German rulers to send soldiers The twist: As many as 40 to 50 of every 100 never went home. Some died. Some stayed in America.

On December 26, 1776, American soldiers marched into Trenton, New Jersey. It was early in the morning. They had crossed the icy Delaware River in the night. The soldiers guarding Trenton were not British. They were Germans. Their leader was Colonel Johann Rall. The Americans captured about 900 of them. Rall was shot and died.

Why Germans Fought for Britain

Americans called these soldiers Hessians. Most of them came from Hesse-Kassel. That was one of many small states in Germany. Germany was not one country then. Each state had its own ruler and its own army.

Britain did not have enough soldiers for the war. So the king made deals with German rulers. He paid each ruler money. In return, the ruler sent his soldiers to America. The soldiers did not get to choose. Many were drafted. That means they were made to join the army. Others joined for the pay.

About 30,000 German soldiers came to America. At times one of every three soldiers in the British army in America was German.

Where They Fought

The first Germans landed in New York in August 1776. They fought at Long Island that month. They fought in almost every big battle after that. Some marched with General Burgoyne from Canada. They gave up with him at Saratoga in 1777. Others fought in the South in 1781.

Germans taken in battle were prisoners of war. A prisoner of war is a captured soldier. Many of the Trenton prisoners were sent to Lancaster, Pennsylvania. Many farmers there spoke German.

Many Never Went Home

About 7,500 German soldiers died in America. Most died of sickness, not in battle. Thousands more stayed when the war ended. Many settled near German farmers in Pennsylvania. The rest sailed home after the peace in 1783.

Baroness von Riedesel’s journal — the cellar at Saratoga October 1777; translated 1867

Frederika von Riedesel wrote this in her journal. A journal is a diary. She was a noble lady from Germany. Her husband led the German troops. They were part of General Burgoyne's army. She followed him to America. She brought their three small girls. In October 1777, near Saratoga, she hid in a cellar. American guns were hitting the house. She wrote the journal for her family at home. It was put into English in 1867.

At last we had to hide in a cellar. I lay down in a corner not far from the door. My children lay on the dirt floor with their heads in my lap. We spent the whole night that way. […] Eleven cannon balls went through the house. We could plainly hear them rolling over our heads. One poor soldier was lying on a table. The doctors were about to cut off his leg. Then another cannon ball took off his other leg, right in the middle of the operation.

Before dawn on December 26, 1776, about 2,400 American soldiers with eighteen cannons finished crossing the ice-choked Delaware River and marched south to Trenton, New Jersey. The garrison there was German, commanded by Colonel Johann Rall, a career officer who had fought at Long Island and White Plains that year and who had been warned that his post was exposed. After about two hours of fighting the Americans held the town. The Germans lost 22 men killed and 86 wounded, and close to 900 were taken prisoner. Rall was mortally wounded while trying to rally his men. The Americans had only five men wounded.

Why Germans Fought for Britain

Americans called all of the German soldiers Hessians, because more than half of them, about 18,000 to 19,000 men, came from the state of Hesse-Kassel. The rest came from Brunswick, Hesse-Hanau, Ansbach-Bayreuth, Waldeck and Anhalt-Zerbst. Germany in the 1700s was a collection of separate states that shared a language but not a government. Each ruler kept his own army, and several of them earned money by renting regiments to larger countries.

Starting in early 1776 Britain signed treaties with these rulers. Under each treaty Britain paid the ruler a subsidy, a yearly payment, in return for his regiments, and the soldiers were known as subsidy troops. Frederick II, the Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel, earned an amount equal to thirteen years of his state's tax revenue from the deal. The soldiers remained in his army and under his officers. The Declaration of Independence accused George III of "transporting large Armies of foreign Mercenaries to compleat the works of death, desolation and tyranny," and the name stuck. A mercenary is a soldier who hires himself out to a foreign army for pay. Most of the Germans had not hired themselves out. Their prince had.

Hesse-Kassel kept its army full by conscription, a system that requires men to serve. Boys were registered for service at age seven, and men between 16 and 30 reported every year to see whether they would be taken. Men without a master or steady work could be taken at any time. Soldiers were paid, the wages were better than farm work, and their families paid lower taxes. Some men were career soldiers, and some volunteered for the pay or the chance to see America. Between 1776 and 1783 about 30,000 German soldiers crossed the Atlantic, and at times they made up about a third of the British army in America.

From Long Island to Guilford Courthouse

The first German regiments landed at New York in August 1776 and fought in the battle of Long Island on August 27. Over the next five years German units served in almost every major campaign. Brunswick troops under General Friedrich Riedesel marched south from Canada with General John Burgoyne and surrendered with him at Saratoga in October 1777. Riedesel's wife, Frederika, had followed him to America with their three small daughters. During the retreat before the surrender she sheltered with wounded men in a cellar while American guns fired on the house. "Eleven cannon balls went through the house," she wrote in her journal, "and we could plainly hear them rolling over our heads." In the South, the Hessian Regiment von Bose fought at Guilford Courthouse, North Carolina, in March 1781.

Germans captured in battle were held as prisoners of war, soldiers kept by the enemy until they were exchanged or the war ended. Most of the prisoners were held around Lancaster, Pennsylvania. The site was chosen because it was far from the fighting and because much of the countryside around it spoke German. Some prisoners were later exchanged for Americans held by the British. Some slipped away from the prison towns and stayed.

Many Never Went Home

Between 40 and 50 percent of the German soldiers never returned to Germany. About 7,500 died, but only about 1,200 of them died in combat, and disease killed the rest. Thousands more deserted during the war or stayed behind when it ended. Estimates of how many settled in America range from about 3,000 to 6,000. Many took up farms among the German-speaking communities of Pennsylvania. The regiments that remained sailed for home as peace came in 1783, and the Riedesels sailed with them.
