Indentured Servants

Working Off the Cost of the Voyage

Who: Mostly poor people from Europe who signed a contract to work for years How many: Tens of thousands during the Revolution (about 2 or 3 of every 100 workers) The contract: Usually 4 to 7 years; no marriage without permission, no land, could be bought and sold Key tension: Caught in a war for "freedom" while not free themselves

In 1750 a ship landed at Philadelphia. It was full of poor families from Germany. The trip had taken at least seven weeks. A passenger named Gottlieb Mittelberger watched what happened next. People who could pay for the trip walked off the ship. The rest had to stay on board. Buyers came out from the city. They looked over the healthy passengers. Then they made a deal. Each person would work for years to pay for the trip.

What an Indenture Was

The people sold on that ship became indentured servants. An indentured servant signed a paper. The paper said he or she would work for a master for a set number of years. Most terms ran four to seven years. Children had to work until they turned 21. Some parents sold their children's years so they could go free themselves.

During those years, a servant was not free. A servant could not marry without the master's say-so. The master could sell the servant's contract to someone else. A servant who ran away and got caught had years added on. At the end of the term, the servant went free. The master had to give freedom dues. Freedom dues were a payment at the end, like new clothes or a cow.

Servants in a War for Liberty

By 1775, tens of thousands of people were indentured servants. That was about 2 or 3 of every 100 workers. Then the war began. Servants still owed years of work. On November 7, 1775, Lord Dunmore made an offer. He was the king's governor of Virginia. He said servants of rebels could go free if they fought for the king. Some servants ran to the British. Some joined the American army instead. Many more stayed and worked off their years.

After the War

Few ships of immigrants crossed the ocean during the war. So the trade in servants almost stopped. After the peace in 1783, the ships came back. Servants were sold on the docks again. But the trade never got as big as before. By 1800 it was almost gone.

Mittelberger — the ship becomes a market Philadelphia, 1750; published 1756

Gottlieb Mittelberger wrote this. He came from Germany to Philadelphia by ship in 1750. He saw what happened to the poor passengers when the ship landed. He went home and wrote a book about the trip. It came out in 1756. He wrote it to warn people who were thinking of making the trip.

Simplified version, not the source. These are not the writer’s words, so do not quote them. To quote, use the original words below.

Every day, English, Dutch and German people come from the city of Philadelphia and other places. […] They pick out the healthy people they think will fit their business. Then they make a deal with them. The deal says how long each one will work to pay for the trip. […] Grown-ups sign a paper. They agree to work 3, 4, 5 or 6 years for the money they owe. It depends on their age and strength. But very young people, from 10 to 15 years old, must work until they are 21.

Many parents must sell and trade away their children like cattle. If the children take on the debt, the parents can leave the ship free. […] It often happens that parents and children leave the ship and do not see each other again for many years. Some may never see each other again in their whole lives.

In 1750 a ship full of German families tied up at the docks of Philadelphia after a voyage of at least seven weeks. A passenger named Gottlieb Mittelberger watched what happened next. The people who could pay for their passage, or find someone to guarantee it, walked off the ship. The rest had to stay on board until someone bought their time. Each day, buyers came out from Philadelphia and the surrounding country and looked over the passengers. They chose the healthy ones first, Mittelberger wrote, so "the sick always fare the worst." Then they bargained with each passenger over how many years of work would pay for the trip.

What an Indenture Was

The people sold on that ship became indentured servants, workers bound by a written contract, called an indenture, to serve a master for a set number of years. Mittelberger wrote that adults bound themselves "to serve 3, 4, 5 or 6 years for the amount due by them, according to their age and strength," and that children between 10 and 15 "must serve till they are 21 years old." Most terms ran about four to seven years. Parents who could not pay sometimes had to sell their children's labor so that they themselves could leave the ship free, and Mittelberger wrote that such families often did "not see each other again for many years, perhaps no more in all their lives." He went home to Germany in 1754 and in 1756 published a book warning his countrymen against the voyage.

During the term, a servant's time belonged to the master. A servant could not marry without the master's permission, and the master could sell the contract, and the servant with it, to someone else. Courts added time to the terms of runaways who were caught. In Cumberland County, Pennsylvania, a servant named Edmund Carmaddy ran away from his master on September 11, 1775, was caught in Maryland on February 3, 1776, and was jailed in Carlisle. On April 8, 1776, the court ordered him to serve two more years beyond the time in his indenture. At the end of a term the servant went free and received freedom dues, a payment the master owed when the contract ended. In October 1775 the same county's court ordered a master to give his former servant a new coat, a new hat, and a three-year-old heifer.

Servants in a War for Liberty

Between the 1630s and the Revolution, one-half to two-thirds of the white immigrants to the thirteen colonies arrived under an indenture. By 1775 the system had shrunk, and indentured servants made up only about 2 or 3 percent of the colonial labor force, tens of thousands of people in all. Most were poor immigrants from Europe, and in Pennsylvania many were German. Their status was different from slavery in one way that mattered: the indenture ended, and the servant went free.

When the war began in 1775, servants had to decide what to do with the years they still owed. On November 7, 1775, Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, issued a proclamation that declared "all indented Servants, Negroes, or others, (appertaining to Rebels,) free that are able and willing to bear Arms" for the king. The offer was made to the servants of rebels, not to the servants of Loyalists. Some servants ran to the British lines. Others enlisted in the Continental Army, sometimes as a substitute, a man who served in place of another, with the master's permission and a promise that the contract would end early. Many more stayed and served out their terms.

After the War

The war cut off the trade. Few ships of immigrants crossed the Atlantic between 1775 and 1783, and in Pennsylvania immigration almost stopped. After the peace of 1783 the ships returned, and servants were sold on the Philadelphia docks as before. But the trade never regained its old size. By the end of the 1700s the system of European indentured servitude was dying out.
