James Armistead Lafayette

Enslaved spy whose secrets helped trap the British at Yorktown

Born: Around 1748, Virginia (enslaved) Died: August 9, 1830 (about age 82) Role: Spy who pretended to help the British while helping the Americans Famous for: Secrets that helped win the Battle of Yorktown; freed by Virginia in 1786–87

James Armistead was born a (a person who is owned and made to work for no pay) in Virginia around 1748.

In 1781 he became a (a person who secretly gathers information) for the American army.

James pretended to help the British. They believed him. He even worked for a British general named Cornwallis.

But James was really helping the Americans. He listened to British plans and told them to the American general, Lafayette. He also gave the British false news.

The British did not think a Black man could be a spy. That was their mistake.

James's secrets helped the Americans win the big battle at Yorktown in 1781. That battle ended most of the fighting.

James was not freed right away. In 1786–87, Virginia finally set him free. He added "Lafayette" to his name. He lived as a free farmer and died in 1830.

James Armistead was born into slavery in Virginia around 1748. In 1781, with his enslaver's permission, James volunteered to spy for the Continental Army. He worked under the Marquis de Lafayette, a French general helping the Americans.

James pretended to be a runaway who wanted to help the British. They believed him. The British general Lord Cornwallis even used James as a spy. But James was a double agent: he was really working for the Americans. He listened to British plans and told them to Lafayette. At the same time, he fed the British false information. The British never suspected him. Part of the reason was that they wrongly thought an enslaved Black man could not be a spy.

James's reports helped the Americans and French trap Cornwallis at Yorktown, Virginia. Cornwallis (gave up the fight) on October 19, 1781. That ended most of the fighting. Even so, James was not freed. In 1784, Lafayette wrote a letter praising him. With that letter, James asked Virginia for his freedom, and the state freed him in 1786–87. He added "Lafayette" to his name and became a farmer. Later he got a veteran's (regular payments for past service). He and Lafayette met again in 1824. He died in 1830.

James Armistead was born into slavery in Virginia around 1748, enslaved by William Armistead. In 1781, with his enslaver's permission, he volunteered to spy for the Continental Army under the Marquis de Lafayette. His plan was bold. He posed as a runaway eager to serve the British — and they trusted him so completely that Lord Cornwallis used him to gather (secret military information) on the Americans.

In reality, James was a double agent. He moved through British camps, memorized troop numbers, positions, and plans, and carried them to Lafayette. At the same time, he fed the British carefully chosen false information. The British never suspected him. They assumed an enslaved Black man could not be an effective spy — a prejudice James turned into his advantage. His reports proved vital as American and French forces trapped Cornwallis at Yorktown, where the British surrendered on October 19, 1781.

Freedom did not follow victory. James returned to slavery. Armed with a 1784 (a written statement of praise) from Lafayette, he petitioned the Virginia Assembly, which passed an act freeing him in late 1786, finalized in January 1787. He took the name James Armistead Lafayette, farmed his own land, and eventually won a veteran's pension. In 1824 he shared an emotional reunion with Lafayette during the general's farewell tour. He died in 1830.

James Armistead — born into slavery in Virginia around 1748 — became one of the Revolution's most consequential intelligence agents by weaponizing the very prejudice that bound him. In 1781, with his enslaver's consent, he joined the Marquis de Lafayette's command and posed as a runaway offering his services to the British; Lord Cornwallis, certain that an enslaved man posed no threat, used him as a scout. James was in fact a double agent, ferrying accurate British troop (positions and arrangements) to Lafayette while seeding Cornwallis's camp with (deliberately false information).

His intelligence helped the allied American and French armies pin Cornwallis at Yorktown, whose surrender on October 19, 1781 effectively decided the war. Lafayette later certified his value in a 1784 testimonial, writing that James had done "essential services to me while I had the honour to command in this state" (Lafayette, 1784). Yet victory bought him nothing: Virginia's 1782 emancipation law rewarded enslaved men who had served as soldiers, not as spies, so James remained enslaved until he petitioned the Assembly, which freed him by an act passed in late 1786 and finalized in January 1787.

He took the name James Armistead Lafayette, farmed in New Kent County, and in 1818 secured a state pension for his service — recognition that came grudgingly and late. When Lafayette toured America in 1824, the two men reunited, and a celebrated portrait fixed James's likeness for history. His life poses the Revolution's central question in its sharpest form: what did "liberty" mean in a republic that a man could help create and still not be free within?
