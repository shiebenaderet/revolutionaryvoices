Mercy Otis Warren

A Patriot With a Pen

Born: September 25, 1728, Barnstable, Massachusetts Died: October 19, 1814 (age 86) Role: Playwright, writer, historian, advisor Famous for: Plays that made fun of British leaders; her 1805 history of the Revolution

On March 26, 1772, a Boston newspaper printed part of a play. The play was set in a faraway land. A greedy ruler named Rapatio ran it. No author's name was printed. But readers in Boston knew who Rapatio was. He was Thomas Hutchinson, the royal governor of Massachusetts. The writer was a mother of five from Plymouth. Her name was Mercy Otis Warren.

Learning With Her Brothers

Mercy Otis was born in 1728 in Barnstable, Massachusetts. Girls then did not go to college. Her father let her study with her brothers. Their uncle was their tutor. A tutor is a teacher who teaches at home. Her brother James Otis Jr. became a lawyer. In 1761 he argued in court against British searches of ships and homes. In 1764 he wrote that Britain could not tax the colonies without their consent.

Plays Against the Governor

In 1754 Mercy married James Warren. He was a merchant and a lawmaker. They had five sons. Patriot leaders like John Adams met at their house in Plymouth.

In the 1770s Warren wrote plays that made fun of the governor and his friends. This kind of writing is called satire. Satire uses humor to attack people in power. Her plays were printed in newspapers. They were meant to be read, not acted. Most people then thought politics was not a woman's business. So Warren left her name off her plays. Patriot leaders knew she wrote them. She wrote letters to John Adams. She became a close friend of Abigail Adams.

After the War

In 1788 the states were voting on the new Constitution. Warren wrote a pamphlet against it. She said it needed a bill of rights. She signed it "A Columbian Patriot." For many years people thought a man wrote it.

In 1805 Warren published a history of the Revolution in three books. She was one of the first women to write a history of the war. In the book she wrote about John Adams. He did not like what she said. He sent her angry letters in 1807. They did not write to each other for five years. In 1812 they made up. Mercy Otis Warren died in Plymouth on October 19, 1814. She was 86 years old.

The Constitution and the History

After the war Warren kept writing. In 1788, as the states debated whether to approve the new Constitution, she published a pamphlet called Observations on the New Constitution under the name "A Columbian Patriot." It argued that the Constitution gave the national government too much power and needed a bill of rights. For more than a century, readers believed Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts had written it.

Learning With Her Brothers

Mercy Otis was born on September 25, 1728, in Barnstable, Massachusetts, the daughter of a farmer, lawyer and judge who served in the colonial legislature. Girls then could not attend college, and most received little schooling. Her father let her study with her brothers under their uncle, the Reverend Jonathan Russell, who was their tutor, a teacher who taught them at home and prepared the boys for Harvard. Her brother James Otis Jr. became a lawyer. In 1761 he argued in court against the writs of assistance, papers that let British customs officers search any ship or house for smuggled goods. In 1764 he published a pamphlet, The Rights of the British Colonies Asserted and Proved, which argued that Parliament could not tax the colonists without their consent. In 1769 a customs official struck him on the head during a fight in a Boston coffeehouse, and he never fully recovered.

Plays Against the Governor

In 1754 Mercy married James Warren, a merchant and farmer from Plymouth who had been a friend of her brother at Harvard. He later served as speaker of the Massachusetts House and as president of the Provincial Congress. They had five sons. Their house on North Street in Plymouth became a meeting place for Patriots, including John Adams and Samuel Adams. In the 1770s Warren began writing satire, writing that uses humor and exaggeration to attack people in power. Her plays The Adulateur (1772), The Defeat (1773) and The Group (1775) were printed in the Massachusetts Spy and the Boston Gazette and as pamphlets. They were closet dramas, plays meant to be read rather than performed, and Massachusetts had outlawed stage plays in any case. They showed Hutchinson and his supporters as greedy men who sold out their country for the king's favor.

Warren published the plays anonymously, without her name. At the time most people believed politics was not a woman's business, and a woman who wrote about it in public risked her reputation. Patriot leaders knew who wrote the plays. John Adams praised her writing and sent her news from Congress, and she became a close friend of Abigail Adams. The two women exchanged letters for forty years.

The Constitution and the History

After the war Warren kept writing. In 1788, as the states debated whether to approve the new Constitution, she published a nineteen-page pamphlet called Observations on the New Constitution under the name "A Columbian Patriot." It argued that the Constitution gave the national government too much power, allowed a standing army, and had no bill of rights. For more than a century, readers believed Elbridge Gerry of Massachusetts had written it. Historians settled the question in the 1930s from Warren's own letters.

In 1805 Warren published History of the Rise, Progress and Termination of the American Revolution, in three volumes. Hannah Adams had published a shorter account of the war in 1799, and Warren's was the first full history of the war written by a woman. In its opening address she answered those who held that "all political attentions lay out of the road of female life," and she admitted that "the hand often shrunk back from the task; yet ... the work was not relinquished." The book also described John Adams, by then a former president, as a man whose prejudices and passions had sometimes been too strong for his judgment. In July and August 1807 Adams sent her ten angry letters, and the two stopped writing to each other. Their friend Elbridge Gerry helped bring them back together in 1812. Mercy Otis Warren died in Plymouth on October 19, 1814, at the age of 86.
