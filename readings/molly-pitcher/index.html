Molly Pitcher

The Woman at the Cannon

Who: "Molly Pitcher" is a legend likely based on real women, especially Mary Ludwig Hays (born around 1754) Battle: Monmouth, June 28, 1778 (a very hot day) Role: Camp follower who carried water to soldiers Famous for: The story of taking over a cannon when her husband fell

On June 28, 1778, the American army fought the British at Monmouth, New Jersey. It was a very hot day. A soldier named Joseph Plumb Martin stood near the American cannons. He saw a woman at one of the guns. Her husband was on the gun crew. She stayed with him and handed him the gunpowder. A British cannonball flew between her legs. It tore off the bottom of her skirt. She said it was lucky the ball did not fly higher. Then she went back to work.

Women With the Army

Thousands of women followed the American army. Many were soldiers' wives. They were called camp followers. Camp followers cooked, washed clothes, and cared for the sick. They also carried water. No record from the battle says who carried water at Monmouth that day.

The Story of Molly Pitcher

Years after the war, people told a story about a woman at Monmouth. They called her Molly Pitcher. In the story, she carried pitchers of water to the soldiers. Then her husband fell at his cannon. She took his place and kept firing.

No record from the war names a Molly Pitcher. The name first showed up in print about 60 years after the battle. Historians think most of the Molly Pitcher story is legend. A legend is a story passed down for years that cannot be proven.

What the Records Show

Martin wrote about the battle in 1830. He is the only person who saw a woman at a cannon at Monmouth and wrote it down. He did not give her name. He did not say she carried water. He did not say her husband fell.

One woman did take her husband's place at a cannon. Her name was Margaret Corbin. It happened at Fort Washington, New York, in 1776. Her husband was killed at his gun. She stepped in and fired it. She was badly hurt. In 1779 Congress gave her half of a soldier's pay for life.

Mary Ludwig Hays

Most people say Molly Pitcher was Mary Ludwig Hays. She was born around 1754. Her husband William was on a gun crew at Monmouth. Mary followed the army with him. After the war they lived in Carlisle, Pennsylvania. In 1822 the state gave her $40 a year for her service in the war. She died in Carlisle on January 22, 1832.

On June 28, 1778, the Continental Army fought the British at Monmouth Court House, New Jersey, on a day when the temperature neared 100 degrees and men on both sides collapsed from the heat. A Connecticut private named Joseph Plumb Martin stood near the American cannons during the heaviest firing. He watched a woman whose husband served one of the guns. She stayed at the gun with him through the whole battle, handing up cartridges, the paper-wrapped charges of gunpowder that fed the cannon. As she reached for one, a British cannonball passed between her legs and tore away the lower part of her petticoat. Martin wrote that she looked at the damage "with apparent unconcern," remarked that it was lucky the shot had not passed higher, "and continued her occupation."

Women With the Army

The woman Martin saw was one of thousands who followed the Continental Army. Most were soldiers' wives, and many had no other way to live once their husbands enlisted. The army called them camp followers, civilians who traveled with an army and did its daily work. They cooked, washed clothes, nursed the sick and carried water, and the army counted them on its ration rolls. Gun crews needed water too, to swab out the hot barrel of a cannon between shots so that sparks would not set off the next charge. No record from the battle says who carried water at Monmouth that day.

The Story of Molly Pitcher

Years after the war, people told a story about a woman at Monmouth they called Molly Pitcher. In the story, she carried pitchers of water to soldiers collapsing in the heat. When her husband fell at his cannon, she took his place and kept firing until the battle ended. "Molly" was a common nickname for Mary, and "Pitcher" fit the water she carried. No record from the war names a Molly Pitcher. The name first appeared in print in the late 1830s, about 60 years after the battle. In 1848 the printer Nathaniel Currier sold a picture titled "Molly Pitcher, the Heroine of Monmouth," and prints like it fixed the story in the public mind.

Historians think most of the Molly Pitcher story is legend, a story passed down for years that cannot be proven. Many describe the figure as a composite, a single character built from the deeds of several real women. They disagree on how much is real. Some accept that Mary Hays was the woman at the gun. The historian Ray Raphael argues that the Monmouth story cannot be traced to any record from the war and was attached to her only in the 1800s.

What the Records Show

Martin published his memoir in 1830 under the title A Narrative of Some of the Adventures, Dangers and Sufferings of a Revolutionary Soldier. It is the only account of a woman at a cannon at Monmouth written by someone who was there. He gave no name, said nothing about water, and did not say her husband fell. The only other early account, from an army surgeon named Albigence Waldo, was secondhand and survives only in a quotation printed in 1927. Every other detail of the story was added later, in the 1800s.

One woman at a cannon is well documented, at a different battle. On November 16, 1776, at Fort Washington in New York, Margaret Corbin's husband John was killed at his gun. She took his place and kept firing until grapeshot tore her arm and chest. She lost the use of her left arm. On July 6, 1779, Congress granted her half a soldier's pay for life, the first military pension, money paid regularly for past service, that the United States gave to a woman.

Mary Ludwig Hays

The woman most often named as Molly Pitcher is Mary Ludwig Hays. Most sources give her birth date as October 13, 1754, though some dispute it. Her husband William Hays, a barber, served as a gunner in the 4th Pennsylvania Artillery, and army records place him at Monmouth. Mary followed the army, as many wives did. After the war the couple settled in Carlisle, Pennsylvania. William died in 1786, and Mary later married John McCauley. In 1822 she asked the state of Pennsylvania for a pension as a soldier's widow. On February 21, 1822, the legislature instead granted her $40 a year "for services rendered" in the Revolutionary War. The act did not say what those services were. She died in Carlisle on January 22, 1832, and was buried there under the name Mary McCauley.
