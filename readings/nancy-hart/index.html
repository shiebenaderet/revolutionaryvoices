Nancy Hart

The Cabin on the Broad River

Born: Around 1735 (North Carolina or Pennsylvania) Died: Around 1830 Role: Woman living on the edge of settled land in Georgia; Patriot Famous for: The legend of capturing Loyalist soldiers in her cabin; Hart County, Georgia, is named for her

In December 1912, workers were building a railroad in Elbert County, Georgia. They dug up six skeletons. The bones lay in a neat row. They had been in the ground for about 100 years. The land was near the place where Nancy Hart's log cabin once stood. For almost 90 years, people in Georgia had told a story about that cabin.

A Frontier Family

Nancy Hart was born around 1735, probably in North Carolina. In the early 1770s she and her husband Benjamin moved to Georgia. They settled near the Broad River. This was the frontier. The frontier was the edge of the land where settlers lived.

During the war, Benjamin Hart was an officer in the Georgia militia. In Georgia the war split neighbors. Some backed the king. They were called Loyalists. Others wanted independence. The two sides attacked each other's farms.

The Story of the Cabin

The story says that five or six Loyalist soldiers came to the cabin. Benjamin was away. The soldiers shot Nancy's turkey. They told her to cook it. She fed them and gave them wine. She sent her daughter to get water. She told her to blow a shell horn to call for help.

The soldiers set their guns by the wall. As they ate, Nancy slid the guns out through a gap in the logs. The men saw what she was doing. She grabbed a gun and pointed it at them. She shot one man and then another. Then Benjamin and the neighbors came. The story says they hanged the soldiers who were left.

Stories also say Nancy was six feet tall with red hair. They say she was a great shot. Some say she spied on Loyalist camps dressed as a man.

How the Story Spread

No one wrote the cabin story down during the war. It was first printed in a Georgia newspaper in 1825. That was about 50 years later. In 1848 a writer named Elizabeth Ellet put Nancy in a book about women of the Revolution. People all over the country read it.

Historians cannot prove the story. The details grew each time it was told. Records do show that a Hart family lived on the Broad River. The six skeletons fit the story. But they do not prove it. No one knows who the six people were.

Later Years

After the war the Harts moved to the Georgia coast. Later Nancy moved to Kentucky. She died there around 1830. In 1853 Georgia named Hart County after her. It is the only county in Georgia named for a woman.

A Frontier Family

Nancy Ann Morgan Hart was born around 1735, probably in North Carolina, though some accounts say Pennsylvania and some put her birth as late as 1747. In the early 1770s she and her husband Benjamin Hart moved with their children to Georgia and settled in the valley of the Broad River, in what was then Wilkes County and is now Elbert County. This was the frontier, the edge of the land where settlers lived. During the Revolution Benjamin Hart served as a lieutenant in the Georgia militia under Colonel Elijah Clarke, who led the Patriot militia of the backcountry.

In the Georgia backcountry the Revolution was a civil war. Neighbors who supported the king, called Loyalists or Tories, fought neighbors who supported independence, called Patriots or Whigs. After the British captured Savannah in December 1778 and Augusta in January 1779, Loyalist and Patriot bands raided each other's farms across the backcountry. The area was so violent that the British called it the Hornet's Nest.

The Story of the Cabin

According to the story, five or six Loyalist soldiers came to the Hart cabin one day while Benjamin was away. One of them shot her prize turkey and ordered her to cook it. She fed them and opened her jugs of wine. As they ate and drank, she sent her daughter Sukey to the spring for water and told her to blow a conch shell to call the neighbors. The soldiers had stacked their muskets by the wall. Nancy began slipping them, one at a time, out through a gap in the chinking between the logs. When the men noticed, she pointed a musket at them. She shot one man who moved toward her and then a second. Benjamin and the neighbors arrived, and the story says the surviving soldiers were hanged.

Other stories say she spied on Loyalist camps disguised as a simple-minded man, and that she threw boiling soap water through a crack in the wall at a Loyalist spy who was peeking in. Accounts from the 1800s describe her as six feet tall, red-haired and cross-eyed, with a face scarred by smallpox, and an excellent shot with a rifle.

How the Story Spread

No one wrote the cabin story down during the war. It first appeared in print on October 6, 1825, in the Milledgeville Southern Recorder, a Georgia newspaper, about fifty years after the events, and the Savannah Republican reprinted it. In 1848 the writer Elizabeth Ellet included Nancy Hart in her book The Women of the American Revolution, which sold across the country. Ellet praised and mocked her in the same paragraph. She called Hart "a zealous lover of liberty" and "a woman entirely uneducated, and ignorant of all the conventional civilities of life," and she wrote that Hart had "a woman's heart for her friends, though that of a tigress ... for the enemies of her country."

Historians cannot prove the story. In 1955 the historian E. Merton Coulter published a study in the Georgia Historical Quarterly that traced the story from the 1825 newspaper through later retellings and showed that it gained new details each time. Ellet herself was writing sixty-five years after the war, from stories that had been passed down in Georgia. Records do show that a Hart family lived on the Broad River and that Benjamin Hart served in the militia. The six skeletons found in 1912 fit the story, but they do not prove it. No one recorded who the six people were, how they died, or whether anyone examined the bones.

Later Years

In the late 1790s the Harts moved to Brunswick, on the Georgia coast. After Benjamin died, Nancy moved back to the Broad River for a time and then lived near Athens, in Clarke County. Around 1803 she moved to Henderson County, Kentucky, and she died there around 1830. In 1853 Georgia named Hart County for her, with its county seat at Hartwell. It is the only county in the state named for a woman.
