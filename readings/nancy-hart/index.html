Nancy Hart

Georgia Patriot whose famous stories are mostly legend

Born: Around 1735 (North Carolina or Pennsylvania) Died: Around 1830 Role: Woman living on the edge of settled land in Georgia; Patriot Famous for: The legend of capturing Loyalist soldiers in her cabin; Hart County, Georgia, is named for her

Nancy Hart was a frontier woman (living at the edge of settled land) in Georgia during the Revolutionary War. She was tall and known as a great shot with a rifle.

There is a famous story about her. The story says some Loyalist soldiers (men who supported the British king) came to her cabin and made her cook for them.

The story says Nancy was clever. While the men ate and drank, she slid their guns out through a crack in the wall, one by one. Then she grabbed a gun and held them until help came.

Here is the important part: most of Nancy's story cannot be proven. It was written down many years later. She was likely a real person, but her famous deeds are mostly (a story that may not be fully true).

A county in Georgia is named after her. Her story shows how tough women living on the edge of settled land had to be.

Nancy Hart lived on the Georgia frontier during the Revolutionary War. Stories describe her as tall, red-haired, and an excellent shot with a rifle. In Georgia the war was also a civil war, with Patriot and (colonists who stayed loyal to Britain) neighbors fighting each other.

Her most famous story says that several Loyalist soldiers came to her cabin and demanded a meal. While they ate and drank, Nancy secretly slipped their muskets out through a gap in the cabin wall. When the men noticed, she held them at gunpoint until help arrived.

Nancy Hart is Georgia's most famous Revolutionary heroine — and one of its most legendary. Tradition describes her as a tall, red-haired frontier woman on the Broad River and a crack shot who supported the Patriot cause. In the Georgia backcountry, the Revolution was also a vicious civil war between Patriot and Loyalist neighbors.

Her signature story tells of Loyalist soldiers who forced their way into her cabin and demanded a meal. As they ate and drank, Hart quietly passed their stacked muskets out through a gap in the wall. When they realized it, she held them at gunpoint until her neighbors arrived, shooting one man who rushed her. The captives were then hanged. Other tales cast her as a spy who slipped into British camps disguised as a man.

Historians treat these (daring deeds) with caution. The stories were not written down until decades after the war, and they grew more elaborate with each retelling. A 1955 scholarly study traced how the legend expanded over time. There is a kernel of fact: land and family records indicate a real Hart family on the Georgia frontier. There is also an intriguing piece of physical evidence. In 1912, railroad workers near the cabin site reportedly unearthed a row of six skeletons, some with broken necks. It proves nothing for certain, but it kept the legend alive. Georgia named Hart County for her — the only county in the state named for a woman.

Nancy Hart occupies the borderland between history and folklore, a figure whose fame far outruns the evidence for it. The documentary record supports little more than her existence: land and family records place a Hart family on Georgia's Broad River frontier during the Revolution, in a backcountry where the war devolved into brutal neighbor-against-neighbor violence between Patriots and Loyalists.

The celebrated cabin episode — Hart serving a meal to intruding Tories, smuggling their muskets out through a chinked wall, shooting one and holding the rest for hanging — appears in no contemporary account; it was first published well into the nineteenth century and, as the historian E. Merton Coulter showed in a 1955 Georgia Historical Quarterly study, (gradually added) dramatic detail with each retelling. The legend gained a (a shiver of excitement) of confirmation in 1912, when railroad graders near the old cabin site reportedly uncovered six skeletons, a few with broken necks — suggestive, but far from proof.

What makes Hart worth studying is precisely this gap. A real frontier woman became, in regional memory, "the War Woman": a vehicle for Georgia's pride, for ideas about female toughness, and for the way communities (turn into official heroes) their own. For a podcast, the honest move is to tell the rousing story and then ask what it would take to know whether it happened — and why a young nation needed such a heroine on its southern frontier.
