Native Americans in the Revolutionary War

Native nations had to pick a side in a war over their own land

The cost: The war hurt Native people on every side. The 1783 peace deal ignored Native allies.

Native American nations were put in a terrible spot during the Revolution. Two groups — the British and the American colonists — were fighting over land that belonged to Native peoples. Neither side asked their permission.

Each nation had to choose: help the British, help the Americans, or try to stay (not taking either side).

Most Native nations helped the British. Why? In 1763, Britain had made a rule to stop colonists from moving onto Native land. The colonists wanted that land. So the British seemed like the safer choice.

But the choice split nations apart. The Iroquois Confederacy was six Native nations who had agreed to work together. Some helped the British. Two, the Oneida and Tuscarora, helped the Americans.

The war hurt Native people no matter which side they chose. American soldiers burned dozens of Native towns. And when the war ended, the (the written deal that ended the war) gave Native land to the United States without asking Native nations at all.

Native American nations faced an impossible choice during the Revolution. The British and the American colonists were fighting over land. That land had belonged to Native peoples for thousands of years — and neither side asked permission. Each nation had to decide: help the British, help the Americans, or try to stay (not taking either side).

Most Native nations sided with the British. This wasn't love of British rule — it was strategy. After 1763, Britain had drawn a line (an official rule called the Proclamation of 1763). The line was meant to keep colonists from settling west on Native land. The colonists ignored it and kept coming. So many nations judged that Britain was more likely to protect their land.

The decision tore communities apart. The Iroquois Confederacy had been united for centuries. Now it split: most nations backed Britain, but the Oneida and Tuscarora helped the Americans. The war (destroyed and ruined) Native peoples no matter their choice. In 1779 the Sullivan Expedition burned about 40 Iroquois towns. Worst of all was the 1783 (the official peace agreement) that ended the war. It handed Native land to the United States without consulting the nations who lived there.

For Native American nations, the Revolution was a war fought over their homelands by two groups of outsiders. Neither side recognized Native rights. The nations had to choose among bad options: ally with Britain, ally with the Americans, or attempt a neutrality that both sides would violate.

Most Native warriors who fought sided with Britain. That was a strategic judgment, not affection. After the Seven Years' War, the Proclamation of 1763 had drawn a line along the Appalachian Mountains barring colonial settlement to the west. The line was weakly enforced, but it signaled at least (in name only) British respect for Native territory. Land-hungry colonists openly ignored it. The choice of sides shattered alliances. The Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee), united for generations, split apart. Most of its nations backed Britain, but the Oneida and Tuscarora aided the Americans — even bringing food to Valley Forge.

The war devastated Native communities regardless of allegiance. In 1779 the Sullivan Expedition, ordered by Washington, burned some forty Iroquois towns and their crops. In 1782, Pennsylvania militia (brutally killed) ninety-six peaceful, Christian Delaware (Lenape) people at Gnadenhütten. The final betrayal came at the end of the war. The 1783 Treaty of Paris transferred vast Native homelands to the United States without consulting — or even mentioning — the nations who had lived there and fought in the war. For Native peoples, American independence meant accelerating loss.

To Native American nations, the American Revolution was someone else's civil war waged across their (self-governing) land — a contest in which both parties claimed territory that belonged to neither. Forced to wager their futures, most nations that took up arms sided with Britain, and the logic was coldly sound: the Crown's Proclamation of 1763 had at least drawn a line against westward settlement, whereas the colonists embodied the very land hunger that line was meant to restrain.

The war fractured Native (organized nations and governments) from within. The Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) Confederacy — a six-nation league of remarkable longevity — broke apart, with the Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga generally siding with Britain while the Oneida and Tuscarora supported the Americans, scouting for the Continental Army and carrying provisions to Valley Forge. Neutrality, attempted by nations like the Delaware, proved impossible to hold. And loyalty earned no protection: Washington's 1779 Sullivan Expedition torched roughly forty Iroquois towns to break the Confederacy's capacity to fight, and in 1782 Pennsylvania militia murdered ninety-six unarmed Christian Delaware at Gnadenhütten.

The deepest betrayal was diplomatic: the 1783 Treaty of Paris ceded sovereignty over enormous Native homelands to the new United States without consulting, or even naming, the nations who inhabited them. For Native America, the Revolution did not extend liberty; it removed a check on expansion, opening a century of (having land and homes taken away). A podcast that centers their experience reveals the Revolution's other face — independence for some, built on the coming conquest of others.
