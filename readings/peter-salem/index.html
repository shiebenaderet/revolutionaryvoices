Peter Salem

Formerly enslaved soldier who fought at Bunker Hill and through the Revolution

Born: Around 1750, Framingham, Massachusetts (enslaved) Died: August 16, 1816 (about age 66) Role: Continental Army soldier Famous for: People say he shot British Major Pitcairn at Bunker Hill; he served a long time

Peter Salem was born a (a person who is owned and made to work for no pay) in Massachusetts around 1750. The family who owned him freed him so he could join the army.

Peter became a soldier in the American army. He was one of thousands of Black men who fought in the Revolution.

He fought at the Battle of Bunker Hill in 1775. People say Peter shot a British leader named Major Pitcairn. (We are not fully sure who fired the shot.)

Peter kept fighting for years. Being a Black soldier was hard. Some white soldiers did not want him there. But Peter was brave and kept serving.

After the war, Peter was free but poor. He made woven seats for chairs. He died in 1816.

Peter showed that Black Americans fought for freedom from the very start.

Peter Salem was born into slavery in Framingham, Massachusetts, around 1750. When he was about 25, his enslaver freed him so he could join the Continental Army. Thousands of African American men fought for American independence. Peter became one of them.

On June 17, 1775, Peter fought at the Battle of Bunker Hill. By (a story passed down over time), he shot and killed a British officer, Major John Pitcairn. But historians are not sure who fired the shot. Another Black soldier, Salem Poor, was also praised that day.

Peter Salem was born into slavery around 1750 in Framingham, Massachusetts. He took his name from the family that enslaved him. As a young man he was freed so he could enlist. That was a common arrangement in Massachusetts, where some enslavers sent formerly enslaved men to fight in their place. Peter became one of the thousands of African Americans who served in the Continental Army.

At the Battle of Bunker Hill on June 17, 1775, Peter fought in one of the war's bloodiest early battles. Tradition credits him with firing the shot that (wounded so badly he died) British Major John Pitcairn — a story repeated in art and history for two centuries. But the claim is genuinely uncertain. British accounts describe Pitcairn being hit by several shots. Another Black soldier, Salem Poor, was praised for heroism in the same fight and is sometimes confused with Peter. What is not in doubt is that Peter was there and fought bravely.

Peter served through several years of the war. He is traditionally said to have fought at Saratoga and Stony Point. Like many veterans — and especially Black veterans facing prejudice — he struggled afterward. He worked as a cane weaver and died poor in 1816. His life is a reminder that the Revolution's soldiers were far more diverse than the textbooks long showed.

Peter Salem — born into slavery around 1750 in Framingham, Massachusetts, and named for the family that owned him — was freed to take up arms, one of many enslaved New Englanders sent to fight in an enslaver's stead. He stood among the several thousand African Americans who served the Patriot cause, a presence the young republic would spend generations forgetting.

At Bunker Hill on June 17, 1775, Peter earned lasting fame for supposedly shooting British Major John Pitcairn as the officer mounted the (a small earthen fort). The story is powerful but contested: the early historian Samuel Swett credited Salem, yet British sources report Pitcairn struck by multiple balls, and the heroics of another Black soldier, Salem Poor — formally commended by fourteen officers as "a brave and gallant soldier" (petition to the General Court, 1775) — were often blurred into Peter's. Who fired the fatal shot cannot be settled; that Black soldiers fought conspicuously at Bunker Hill can.

Peter reenlisted across several campaigns and, by tradition, served at Saratoga and Stony Point before (leaving army service) to a hard freedom. He wove cane chair seats, slid into poverty, and died in a Framingham (a charity home for the poor) in 1816 — a fate that exposed how little the nation he helped found would do for its Black veterans. The decades-long uncertainty over his single famous shot is itself revealing: the record-keepers of the era simply did not trouble to document the lives of men like him with care.
