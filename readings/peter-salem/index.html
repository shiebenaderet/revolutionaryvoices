Peter Salem

A Minuteman at Bunker Hill

Born: Around 1750, Framingham, Massachusetts (enslaved) Died: August 16, 1816 (about age 66) Role: Continental Army soldier Famous for: People say he shot British Major Pitcairn at Bunker Hill; he served a long time

On April 19, 1775, the minutemen of Framingham, Massachusetts, marched toward Concord. British soldiers were on the road from Boston. One of the Framingham men was Peter Salem. A few weeks before, he had been enslaved.

Freed to Join the Army

Salem was born into slavery around 1750 in Framingham. A person in slavery is owned by someone else and works for no pay. His first owner was Jeremiah Belknap. In early 1775 Belknap sold him to Lawson Buckminster. Buckminster led the Framingham minutemen. Minutemen were farmers who promised to be ready to fight in a minute. Buckminster set Salem free so he could join. No one knows for sure where the name Salem came from.

Bunker Hill

After Concord, the American soldiers gathered around Boston. On the night of June 16, 1775, they dug a small dirt fort on Breed's Hill. The next day the British attacked it. This fight is called the Battle of Bunker Hill. Salem fought in the fort. The Americans ran out of gunpowder. Then the British took the hill. But more than 1,000 British soldiers were killed or hurt.

A British officer named John Pitcairn was killed in the attack on the fort. In 1818 a writer said that "a black soldier named Salem" shot him. Other men said they fired the shot. No one knows for sure who did. Another Black soldier, Salem Poor, fought there too. People sometimes mix up the two men.

Five Years a Soldier

Salem joined the army again and again. In 1776 he fought at Trenton. In 1777 he was at Saratoga. In 1779 he was at Stony Point. His service ended in March 1780. He had been a soldier for almost five years.

After the War

In 1783 Salem married Katy Benson. Later he lived in Leicester, Massachusetts. He built a cabin there. He wove cane seats for chairs and made baskets. He was poor. He died in Framingham on August 16, 1816. The town paid for his grave. In 1882 the town put up a stone for him. It says "A Soldier of the Revolution."

Nell, The Colored Patriots of the American Revolution Published 1855

William C. Nell was a Black writer who worked to end slavery. In 1855 he wrote a book about Black soldiers in the Revolution. White writers had left those soldiers out, and Nell wanted to put them back in the record. Here he quotes Samuel Swett, who wrote a history of Bunker Hill in 1818. Nell wrote 80 years after the battle. He uses the word "colored," the usual word for Black people in his time.

Simplified version, not the source. These are not the writer’s words, so do not quote them. To quote, use the original words below.

Swett wrote a book about the Battle of Bunker Hill. In it he tells of a Black man in that fight. He says: "[…] He was at Bunker Hill. A historian says that Major Pitcairn climbed up the fort wall. Pitcairn shouted, "The day is ours!" Then a Black soldier named Salem shot him, and he fell. […] The men in the army gave money to the Black soldier. They took him to Washington and said he was the one who did it."

Salem was not the only one. Many Black soldiers fought at Bunker Hill. Titus Coburn, Alexander Ames and Barzilai Lew came from Andover. Cato Howe came from Plymouth. Each of them later got a pension. A pension is money paid to an old soldier.

Nell's footnote: In some old prints of the battle, this Black soldier stands in a clear spot. But in newer copies, he is gone. Nell says this is an important thing to leave out, and a shameful one. […]

On the morning of April 19, 1775, the minutemen of Framingham, Massachusetts, marched toward Concord. Word had come that British soldiers were on the road from Boston. One of the men in Captain Simon Edgell's company was Peter Salem. A few weeks earlier he had been enslaved.

Freed to Enlist

Salem was born into slavery around 1750 in Framingham. His first enslaver was Jeremiah Belknap, who sold him early in 1775 to Lawson Buckminster. Buckminster was a major who commanded Framingham's minutemen, farmers who promised to be ready to fight at a minute's notice. He freed Salem so that Salem could enlist. Some other enslavers in Massachusetts also freed men so they could serve. No one knows for sure where the name Salem came from. It was not the name of either family that had owned him.

The Fort on Breed's Hill

After Lexington and Concord, the militia gathered around Boston, and Salem joined Colonel John Nixon's regiment. On the night of June 16, 1775, the Americans dug a redoubt, a small fort made of packed earth, on Breed's Hill above Charlestown. The next day the British attacked it. The fight is known as the Battle of Bunker Hill. The Americans drove back two attacks, but each man had only about thirteen cartridges. When the gunpowder ran out, the British took the hill. More than 1,000 British soldiers were killed or wounded that day.

Major John Pitcairn of the British marines was killed in the attack on the redoubt. In 1818 Samuel Swett wrote a history of the battle that said "a black soldier named Salem shot him through, and he fell." Other men also claimed the shot, and one early account said four musket balls hit Pitcairn. Historians cannot say who fired the shot that killed him. Another Black soldier, Salem Poor, fought at Bunker Hill that day, and his officers later praised him in a letter to the Massachusetts government. The two men are sometimes confused.

Five Years a Soldier

Salem enlisted again and again. In 1776 he fought at Harlem Heights and Trenton. In 1777 he signed up for three more years and was at Saratoga. In 1779 he was at Stony Point, where American light infantry stormed a British fort on the Hudson River at night. His service ended on March 1, 1780, almost five years after he marched to Concord.

A Cabin in Leicester

In September 1783 Salem married Katy Benson. He later settled in Leicester, Massachusetts, where he built a cabin and earned a living weaving cane seats for chairs, making baskets and mending chairs. He grew old poor. He died in Framingham on August 16, 1816. The town buried him in a pauper's grave, a grave paid for by the town because he had no money. In 1882 the town put a granite monument over it. The stone reads "A Soldier of the Revolution."

On the morning of April 19, 1775, the minutemen of Framingham, Massachusetts, marched toward Concord. Word had come that British soldiers were on the road from Boston. One of the men in Captain Simon Edgell's company was Peter Salem. A few weeks earlier he had been enslaved, and the man who had freed him, Major Lawson Buckminster, commanded the Framingham minutemen.

Freed to Enlist

Salem was born into slavery around 1750 in Framingham, where his mother was also enslaved. His first enslaver was Jeremiah Belknap, who sold him early in 1775 to Buckminster. Buckminster commanded Framingham's minutemen, militia who promised to be ready to fight at a minute's notice, and he freed Salem so that Salem could enlist. A Massachusetts historian of the 1800s, Emory Washburn, wrote that letting an enslaved man enlist "worked a practical emancipation." Framingham's town historian wrote that Belknap and Buckminster later gave the town a bond promising to support Salem for the rest of his life. No one knows for sure where the name Salem came from. It was not the name of either family that had owned him.

The Redoubt on Breed's Hill

After Lexington and Concord, the militia gathered around Boston, and Salem joined Captain Thomas Drury's company in Colonel John Nixon's regiment. On the night of June 16, 1775, American soldiers dug a redoubt, a small fort of packed earth, on Breed's Hill above Charlestown. The next day about 2,200 British soldiers attacked it. The fight is known as the Battle of Bunker Hill. The Americans drove back two attacks, but each man had only about thirteen cartridges, and when the powder ran out the British took the hill on their third try. The British won the hill and lost 1,054 men killed or wounded, the heaviest British losses of any battle in the war.

Major John Pitcairn of the British marines was killed in the attack on the redoubt. In 1818 Samuel Swett wrote a history of the battle that said "a black soldier named Salem shot him through, and he fell." In a later edition Swett added a story that soldiers collected money for Salem and presented him to General Washington. Swett wrote more than forty years after the battle. Other men claimed the shot, and in 1787 Jeremy Belknap, a minister and historian, wrote that a Black man from Groton shot him. The only count of the wounds comes from an American clergyman, John Eliot, who wrote that four balls hit Pitcairn. Historians cannot say who fired the shot that killed him, and it is possible that several men did. Another Black soldier, Salem Poor, fought at Bunker Hill that day, and on December 5, 1775, a group of officers signed a petition, a written request to the government, calling him a brave and gallant soldier. The two men are sometimes confused.

Five Years a Soldier

Salem reenlisted again and again. In 1776 he served in the 4th Continental Regiment and fought at Harlem Heights and Trenton. On January 1, 1777, he signed up for three more years in the 6th Massachusetts Regiment. He was at Saratoga in 1777, at Monmouth in 1778 and at Stony Point in 1779, where a light infantry company stormed a British fort on the Hudson River at night under General Anthony Wayne. He was discharged on December 31, 1779, enlisted once more, and ended his service on March 1, 1780, almost five years after he marched to Concord.

A Cabin in Leicester

In September 1783 Salem married Katy Benson. They had no children, and by 1790 they were no longer living together. He settled in Leicester, Massachusetts, where he built a cabin and earned a living weaving cane seats for chairs, making baskets and mending chairs. He grew old poor. He died in Framingham on August 16, 1816, and the town buried him in a pauper's grave, a grave paid for by the town because he had no money. Sources differ on whether he died in the town poorhouse or at a neighbor's house.

In 1855 William C. Nell, a Black historian, printed Swett's account in The Colored Patriots of the American Revolution. In a footnote Nell added that in some early engravings of the battle a Black soldier stood in a prominent place, but that in newer editions his figure was missing. Nell called it "a significant, but inglorious omission." In 1882 the Town of Framingham spent $150 on a granite monument for Salem's grave. The stone reads "A Soldier of the Revolution" and "Erected by the Town, 1882."
