Women in the Revolutionary War

Women and the War for Independence

Roles: Boycotts, running farms, cooking and nursing for the army, spying; a few dressed as soldiers Key idea: Women helped win a freedom they were then denied Watch for: Coverture: a married woman had no legal rights apart from her husband Voices to recover: Daughters of Liberty, Abigail Adams, camp followers

On January 31, 1770, more than 300 women in Boston signed a promise. They would not drink tea. Britain had put a tax on tea. The women wanted the tax to end. A Boston newspaper printed their promise. Women could not vote. But they chose what their families bought.

The Boycotts

Colonists stopped buying British goods. This is called a boycott. A boycott is when people refuse to buy something. Women did most of the shopping. So the boycott needed them. Some women called themselves the Daughters of Liberty. They met to spin thread. They made their own cloth at home.

Running the Farm

The war began in 1775. Men left to fight. Women ran the farms and shops. Abigail Adams ran her family's farm in Massachusetts. Her husband, John Adams, was away at Congress. In 1776 she wrote him a letter. She asked him to "Remember the Ladies" in the new laws. He wrote back that it made him laugh.

With the Army

Thousands of women went with the army. They were called camp followers. A camp follower marched with the soldiers and worked for them. They cooked. They washed clothes. They cared for sick men. The army gave them food. A few women were spies. A very few dressed as men and fought. Deborah Sampson joined the army in 1782. She served for over a year.

The Ladies Association

In 1780 a paper came out in Philadelphia. It was called "The Sentiments of an American Woman." Most historians think Esther Reed wrote it. It asked women to help the soldiers. Reed and other women went door to door. They collected money. Washington asked them to buy cloth. The women sewed more than 2,000 shirts for the soldiers.

After the War

The war did not change the law for women. A married woman lived under coverture. Coverture meant a wife had no legal rights apart from her husband. She could not own land. She could not sign a contract. Women could not vote in any state but one. In New Jersey, women who owned property could vote. In 1807 New Jersey passed a new law. It let only white men vote.

The Sentiments of an American Woman — a broadside Philadelphia, summer 1780

This is a broadside, a printed sheet that was posted and passed around. It came out in Philadelphia in the summer of 1780, while the war was still going on. Most historians think Esther Reed wrote it. It started a big drive. Women went door to door. They raised money for Washington's soldiers. This is how the sheet begins.

When the real war began, the women of America made a firm choice. They would do all they could to help free their country. They love their country with a pure heart. Today they are sad. So far they have given only empty wishes for the Revolution to win. […]

We were "Born for liberty." We are too proud to wear the chains of a cruel government. So we count ourselves with the great rulers of the past. People loved and honored those rulers. They held power over the greatest nations with great glory.

On the commencement of actual war, the Women of America (clearly showed) a firm resolution to contribute as much as could depend on them, to the deliverance of their country. Animated by the purest patriotism, they are sensible of sorrow at this day, in not offering more than (empty; producing nothing) wishes for the success of so glorious a Revolution. […]

Born for liberty, (refusing with pride) to bear the irons of a (unjustly harsh, like a tyrant) Government, we associate ourselves to the grandeur of those Sovereigns, cherished and revered, who have held with so much splendour the scepter of the greatest States.

On January 31, 1770, more than 300 women in Boston signed an agreement. They promised to drink no tea, "sickness excepted," until Parliament repealed the duties it had placed on goods shipped to the colonies, and they promised to refuse tea when it was offered to them. About two weeks later the Boston Evening-Post printed the agreement and reported that it had been signed by "upwards of 300 Mistresses of Families in this Town." Women could not vote or hold office anywhere in the colonies, but they decided what their households bought.

The Boycotts

Beginning with the Stamp Act protests of 1765, colonists answered British taxes with a boycott, an organized refusal to buy British goods. Women made most of the purchases for their households, so no boycott could hold without them. Groups of women who called themselves the Daughters of Liberty held spinning bees, where they spun thread and wove homespun, cloth made at home, to replace imported British cloth.

Women also signed their names in print. In October 1774, 51 women in Edenton, North Carolina, signed a pledge to support the Continental Congress's boycott, and a London newspaper reprinted it. In 1775 a London printer sold a cartoon that made fun of the signers.

Running the Farm

When the fighting began in 1775, men left for the army, and women took over the farms, shops, and family accounts they left behind. The historian Laurel Thatcher Ulrich calls this role the "deputy husband." Under the law a wife normally could not sign contracts or sell land, but in wartime she bought seed, hired workers, paid taxes, and sold crops in her husband's name. Abigail Adams ran the family farm in Braintree, Massachusetts, for most of the war while John Adams served in the Continental Congress and then in Europe. The couple exchanged more than 1,100 letters, which the Massachusetts Historical Society holds today.

On March 31, 1776, Abigail Adams wrote to her husband that the new laws should "Remember the Ladies" and not put "unlimited power into the hands of the Husbands." He wrote back on April 14, "As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh." The Continental Congress never took up the question.

With the Army

Thousands of women traveled with the Continental Army as camp followers, women who marched with the troops and worked for them. Most were the wives of soldiers, and many brought children. They cooked, washed clothes, and nursed sick and wounded soldiers, and the army issued them rations in return. Washington complained about their numbers. In his general orders of August 4, 1777, he wrote that "the multitude of women" with the army were "a clog upon every movement," and he told officers to send away those who were not absolutely necessary.

A few women spied, and a very few served as soldiers in disguise. In 1782 Deborah Sampson of Massachusetts enlisted in the 4th Massachusetts Regiment as "Robert Shurtleff." She served about a year and a half, was wounded, and was discharged with honor in 1783 after a doctor in Philadelphia discovered she was a woman.

The Ladies Association

In June 1780 a broadside, a single printed sheet meant to be posted or passed from hand to hand, appeared in Philadelphia under the title "The Sentiments of an American Woman." It was signed only "An American Woman." Most historians think Esther de Berdt Reed, the wife of Joseph Reed, the head of Pennsylvania's government, wrote it, though the attribution is not certain. The broadside said that from the start of the war "the Women of America manifested a firm resolution to contribute as much as could depend on them, to the deliverance of their country." It proposed that women in every county collect money for the soldiers and send it to Martha Washington.

Reed, Sarah Franklin Bache, and other Philadelphia women formed the Ladies Association on June 10, 1780. They divided the city into districts and went door to door. By July 4 Reed reported to Washington that they had collected more than 300,000 Continental dollars. Reed wanted to give each soldier cash. Washington asked that the money buy linen instead, because he thought some men would spend cash on drink, and the women agreed. They sewed more than 2,000 shirts, and each woman stitched her own name into the shirts she made. Reed died on September 18, 1780, and Bache led the association after her.

After the War

The war did not change women's place in the law. Under coverture, the English legal rule the states kept, a married woman had no legal identity separate from her husband. She could not own property, sign a contract, keep her own wages, or sue in her own name. Single women and widows could own property, and in every state except New Jersey, no woman could vote. New Jersey's 1776 constitution gave the vote to "all inhabitants" of full age who were worth fifty pounds, and women who qualified, mostly widows and unmarried women, cast ballots there. A 1790 state election law even used the words "he or she." In 1807 the New Jersey legislature passed a law that limited the vote to white men who paid taxes.

Historians use the term "republican motherhood," coined by Linda Kerber, for the role the new nation did offer women: raising sons and daughters to be good citizens. Academies for girls opened in Philadelphia in 1787 and in other cities after it, and their founders argued that the mothers of a republic needed an education. New Jersey, the one state where women had voted, did not restore the vote to them until 1920.
